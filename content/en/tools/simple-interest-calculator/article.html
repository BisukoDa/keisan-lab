What this calculator does

Simple interest is interest that is figured only on the original amount of money. That original amount is called the principal. The interest you earn or owe does not get added to the base, so it never earns interest of its own.

This calculator takes a principal, a yearly interest rate and a length of time in years and months. It shows five results:

  • Total interest: the interest for the whole time.
  • Total amount: the principal plus the interest.
  • Interest per full year: the same amount every year, because simple interest is a flat line.
  • Yearly compound total: what the same money would grow to if interest were added once a year.
  • Extra from compounding: the gap between the two methods.

A collapsible table shows both methods side by side at the end of each year.

How to use it

  1. Currency symbol. Pick $, €, £, C$ or A$. This only changes the symbol. Nothing is converted.
  2. Principal. The amount you save, invest or borrow.
  3. Annual interest rate. The yearly rate, from 0% to 100%.
  4. Time: years and extra months. Enter whole years, then extra months from 0 to 11. Three years and six months is entered as 3 and 6.

Results update as you type. The time must be at least one month.

The simple interest formula

The formula is short:

I = P × r × t

I is the interest, P is the principal, r is the yearly rate written as a decimal (4% is 0.04), and t is the time in years. The total amount is then A = P + I.

Months are turned into a part of a year by dividing by 12. Nine months is 9 ÷ 12 = 0.75 year. Six months is 0.5 year. This calculator uses that rule, so the answer for 3 years and 6 months uses t = 3.5.

The comparison column adds interest to the balance once a year. For a part of a year, it adds simple interest on the balance at the start of that year, so a term shorter than one year shows no difference. This is a simplified comparison. Real accounts may compound monthly or daily, and our compound interest calculator lets you try those choices.

Simple interest vs. compound interest

With simple interest, the dollars of interest stay the same every year. The balance grows in a straight line. With compound interest, each year’s interest is added to the balance, so the next year’s interest is larger. The balance grows in a curve that gets steeper over time.

For short times and low rates, the two are close. For long times, compounding wins by a lot. That is good news if you are saving, and bad news if you are borrowing and not paying down the balance.

Where simple interest shows up

  • Some certificates of deposit (CDs), including some brokered CDs, which pay simple interest on the original amount. Check the terms before you buy.
  • Auto loans. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains that a simple interest auto loan calculates interest on the outstanding balance, either daily or monthly. Paying more than the minimum then lowers the interest you pay later. Note that this is a balance-based method, so this calculator’s flat I = P × r × t result will not match a real auto loan schedule. Use our loan calculator for that.
  • Short personal loans and school math problems, where one flat rate applies for the whole time.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate simple interest by hand?
Multiply the principal by the yearly rate (as a decimal) and then by the time in years. For $1,000 at 5% for 4 years, that is 1,000 × 0.05 × 4 = $200.
How do I handle months?
Divide the months by 12 and add them to the years. Eighteen months is 1.5 years. In this calculator, enter 1 year and 6 months.
Can I find the rate or the time instead?
You can rearrange the formula. Rate is r = I ÷ (P × t). Time is t = I ÷ (P × r). This calculator finds the interest and total, so use those formulas on paper for the others.
Is simple interest better for a saver or a borrower?
A saver usually earns less with simple interest than with compound interest at the same rate. A borrower usually pays less. The details depend on the contract.
Why does my lender’s number differ?
Lenders may use daily balances, a 360-day or 365-day year, fees and payment dates. This tool uses the plain formula so you can see the idea clearly.
Does the currency choice change the answer?
No. The symbol is only a label. The math is the same.
